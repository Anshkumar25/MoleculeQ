## 1. The molecular problem

A molecule's chemical behaviour is governed by how its electrons arrange
themselves around its nuclei. The most fundamental quantity derived from that
arrangement is the **electronic energy** — the energy of the electrons in the
field of the (essentially frozen, on this timescale) nuclei. From the
electronic energy as a function of nuclear geometry we can derive equilibrium
bond lengths, vibrational frequencies, reaction barriers, and many other
chemically meaningful properties.

Computing the exact many-electron energy means solving the time-independent
Schrödinger equation for the molecule. This is a **many-body problem**: the
cost of a full configuration-interaction (exact) treatment grows
exponentially with the number of electrons, which is exactly the kind of
scaling that frustrates classical computers for large molecules.

Quantum computers store states in a Hilbert space that grows exponentially
with the number of qubits. This makes them a *natural* candidate for
simulating such many-body quantum systems — an idea traced back to Feynman.

## 2. Why H₂ is the right starting molecule

Hydrogen is the simplest molecule:

- Two nuclei, each a single proton; two electrons in the valence shell,
  i.e. a **2-electron / 2-orbital** problem in a minimal basis.
- Its full electronic-structure problem reduces to just **4 spin-orbital
  modes** in a minimal basis (2 spatial orbitals × 2 spin projections),
  which maps onto **4 qubits** — or **2 qubits** after the standard
  qubit-reduction trick.
- It is small enough that a classical calculation gives the **exact**
  answer (full diagonalization), so we can honestly benchmark the quantum
  estimate.
- It is still a *real* interacting electron problem — the electron–electron
  repulsion and antisymmetry requirement are genuinely present. This is not
  trivially solvable by a single-particle formula.

Everything we build generalises to larger molecules; H₂ is chosen so that
every concept is visible and checkable.

## 3. Basis set

We use the **STO-3G** minimal basis (Slater-type orbitals approximated by 3
Gaussian primitives). For H₂ this yields two atomic orbitals, and the two
spatial orbitals of the molecule are the bonding and anti-bonding linear
combinations (the 1σg / 1σu* molecular orbitals).

STO-3G is a small basis: it does **not** give the exact hydrogen-molecule
energy (that requires many polarization and diffuse functions), but it is the
canonical pedagogical choice, keeps the qubit count minimal, and is precisely
what the reference literature uses. The equilibrium bond length in STO-3G is
slightly different from experiment (~0.99 Å region vs. 0.741 Å exact
experimental); we never present our equilibrium as the *true* molecular
geometry — we present it as the equilibrium *of this model*.

## 4. Chemistry driver

**PySCFDriver** (via `qiskit-nature`) drives the PySCF classical electronic
structure library. PySCF performs the restricted Hartree–Fock (RHF) solve,
computes one- and two-electron integrals, and builds the molecular orbital
basis. `qiskit-nature` packages the result into an
`ElectronicStructureProblem`.

Dependency note: PySCF is a compiled package. The app checks at startup
whether the PySCF driver is importable and, if not, explains clearly that the
chemistry front-end is unavailable (no fake substitute is used).

## 5. Fermion-to-qubit mapping

We use the **Parity mapping** together with **two-qubit reduction**.

- **Jordan-Wigner** would tag each of the 4 spin-orbitals on its own qubit
  (4 qubits). It is conceptually simplest, but wasteful for this problem.
- **Parity mapping** stores the parity of the occupation vector per qubit and,
  when combined with the two-qubit reduction (enforced by knowing the
  particle number and spin), reduces the H₂ problem to just **2 qubits**.

The 2-qubit formulation is the sweet spot for a live demonstration: VQE
runs in milliseconds per iteration, the circuit is small enough to display
and read, and nothing about the physics is lost.

## 6. VQE ansatz

We support two ansätze, both built on the qubit Hamiltonian:

1. **`ry` — hardware-efficient `TwoLocal` ansatz**
   `TwoLocal(num_qubits, rotation_blocks=['ry','rz'], entanglement='cx')`.
   One interleaved parameterized layer between entanglement gates. This is
   the "pen-and-paper" ansatz teachers can write out and explain. It is
   concise and fast, and (for H₂) still reaches the exact answer because the
   ansatz space covers the correct symmetry sector with only a few
   parameters.

2. **`uccsd` — Unitary Coupled-Cluster Singles-Doubles**
   The chemistry expert's ansatz, generated by `qiskit-nature`'s
   `UCCSD` circuit library class from the molecular problem. Single and
   double fermionic excitations are exponentiated to build a unitary that,
   for two electrons in two spatial orbitals, is capable of representing the
   exact ground state.

We default to the **`ry` ansatz** for the interactive dashboard (fast, easy
to read) and expose **`uccsd`** as an option.

## 7. Classical optimizer

- Default: **SLSQP** (Sequential Least-Squares Quadratic Programming) from
  SciPy, bounded, gradient-free-yet-derivative-capable; robust and
  deterministic for small parameter spaces.
- Alternative exposed: **COBYLA** (derivative-free, the Qiskit classic).

Both are supported by `qiskit.algorithms.optimizers` in the installed
ecosystem and are appropriate for the 2-parameter H₂ problem.

## 8. Simulator backend

**Aer `aer_simulator_statevector`** through a `QuantumInstance` / primitive,
and a pure-`statevector` fallback. Statevector simulation is a *genuine*
quantum simulation: the full 2ⁿ-dimensional wavefunction is evolved,
which is exactly the exponential resource that motivates quantum computing
(we just happen to keep it in classical memory because 2² is tiny). The same
VQE code would run on a real backend with only a backend swap.

## 9. Energy convention (critical — do not mix)

Three distinct numbers exist and the interface keeps them labelled:

```
E_elec  : electronic energy            (from VQE / FCI; Ha)
E_nuc   : nuclear repulsion energy     (Z1*Z2 / r for H₂; Ha)
E_total : total molecular energy       = E_elec + E_nuc   (Ha)
```

- **What VQE estimates is E_elec.**
- **The qubit-Hamiltonian exact eigenvalue is the same E_elec.**
- **The classical FCI reference from PySCF is the same E_elec.**
- The *energy curve* shown in the scan uses **E_total** (the physically
  meaningful curve whose minimum marks the model's equilibrium bond length),
  unless a "show electronic energy" toggle is used — never a mixed quantity.

All energies reported in **Hartree (Ha)** and, where a second unit helps,
converted to **electron-volts (eV)** (1 Ha = 27.2114 eV).

## 10. Reference calculation (classical benchmark)

Two complementary classical references:

1. **Exact diagonalization of the qubit Hamiltonian** — `np.linalg.eigh` on
   the dense 2ⁿ×2ⁿ matrix of the parity-mapped qubit operator. This is the
   *same objective function* VQE minimises, so the comparison isolates the
   quality of the *quantum optimisation*, with no basis-set ambiguity. The
   gap `VQE − diag` should be ≈ optimizer tolerance.

2. **PySCF FCI (full configuration interaction)** — the exact electronic
   energy of the *continuous* molecular Hamiltonian in STO-3G. This is the
   true chemistry benchmark; VQE in a complete basis with the right ansatz
   converges to FCI, and STO-3G H₂ has FCI ≈ UCCSD.

**What the comparison does and does not prove**: agreement validates that the
mapped quantum simulation questions the right Hamiltonian and that VQE works.
It does **not** demonstrate quantum advantage — for 2 qubits a laptop
diagonalises exactly and instantly, and that would be true even for
thousands of qubits on classical devices in many practical regimes.

## 11. Expected numbers (to be verified by tests)

At R = 0.735 Å (the textbook STO-3G point, chosen because it is the
literature-standard geometry), STO-3G RHF/FCI H₂ yields approximately:

```
E_elec ≈ -1.1373 Ha       (electronic)
E_nuc  ≈ +0.7144 Ha       (1/r in Bohr)
E_total≈ -0.4229 Ha ... hmm, verify with PySCF output; do not trust memory.
```

**Rule:** no number in this document is authoritative until confirmed by the
live tests in `tests/` — the tests are the source of truth, not this table.
Values in the *app* are always computed at runtime.

## 12. Expected limitations

- STO-3G basis: approximate energies, approximate equilibrium geometry.
- Statevector simulation: exact wavefunction, so it demonstrates the
  **algorithm**, not hardware error/noise. No claim of quantum advantage.
- Single small molecule; the pipeline is not a drug-discovery tool.
- VQE converges to a local (possibly non-global) minimum; results are
  model-dependent (ansatz, optimizer, initialization).
- VQE energy will be ≥ the exact qubit-Hamiltonian eigenvalue by
  construction (variational principle).

## 13. Reproducibility

- Fixed random seed for ansatz-initialization circuits (Qiskit
  transpilation seed) and for optimizer where applicable.
- Deterministic backend (statevector).
- CI-quality assertions in `tests/` guard the numeric conventions.
